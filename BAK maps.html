01 / Maps, Location & Cartographers

Finding Chile and Patagonia on the map

Chile is a long, narrow country on the western edge of South America. It stretches between the Pacific Ocean and the Andes Mountains. This project focuses especially on southern Chilean Patagonia and Torres del Paine National Park, while the maps on this page show the geographic and historical context of the whole country.

1. Where is Chile located?

Chile is located in the southwestern part of South America, along the continent’s Pacific coast. It is in the Southern and Western Hemispheres. Peru borders Chile to the north, Bolivia lies to the northeast, and Argentina borders it to the east. The Pacific Ocean forms most of its western boundary. The country also includes Pacific islands, such as Rapa Nui (Easter Island), and claims a territory in Antarctica. For this project, the latitude range below refers to continental Chile.

3. How long has Chile existed?

The land that is now Chile was inhabited by Indigenous peoples long before the modern country existed. During the colonial period, much of the territory was governed by Spain as part of the Captaincy General of Chile. The independence process began in 1810, and Chile formally declared independence in 1818. The country’s borders and administrative organization continued to develop during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries through treaties, exploration, and political decisions.

Historical maps help show this process. For example, the 1854 map by Claudio Gay represents the young republic, while the 1881 map records boundary proposals between Chile and Argentina in the south. These examples show why maps are historical documents as well as geographic tools.

Historical cartography context: Biblioteca Nacional de Chile, Memoria Chilena, “Representaciones cartográficas de Chile (1768–1929)” and “Representaciones cartográficas del Chile colonial.”

4. A cartographer who influenced Chile: Claudio Gay

Claudio Gay (1800–1873)

Claudio Gay was a French naturalist and scientist who became an important figure in the study and mapping of Chile. In 1830, the Chilean government hired him to explore the country, study its natural resources, and prepare geographic information. Between 1830 and 1842, he traveled from the Atacama region to Chiloé, documenting places that were not yet accurately represented on national maps.

One of his major cartographic accomplishments was the 1841 “Mapa de Chile levantado de orden del Gobierno de esta República.” He also published the Atlas de la historia física y política de Chile, which included a general map and maps of provinces and geographic features. His work helped the government understand and represent the territory of the young republic.

Portrait: historical image of Claudio Gay, via Wikimedia Commons. See the National Library of Chile for information about his cartographic work.

Why was his work important?

Before Gay’s work, Chile lacked a reliable and detailed general map of its territory. His surveys and publications brought together geographic observations about the country and helped establish a more systematic national cartography. Later scientists, including Amado Pissis, continued this work. Pissis was hired in 1848 to study Chile’s geology and mineral resources and published Geografía física de la República de Chile in 1875.

5. Why maps matter in Patagonia

Maps help us understand how the physical environment shapes a region. In Patagonia, the Andes, glaciers, fjords, lakes, rivers, and coastlines create a complex landscape. A physical map shows elevation and landforms, while a political map shows administrative and international boundaries. Satellite images can reveal patterns in the landscape that are harder to see on a traditional map.

Torres del Paine National Park is in the Province of Última Esperanza, Magallanes and Chilean Antarctic Region. Comparing maps of Chile with more detailed maps of Patagonia helps connect the country’s location to the landscapes studied in this project.
