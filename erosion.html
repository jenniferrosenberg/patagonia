03 / Weathering, Mass Wasting & Erosion

How landscapes are reshaped

Patagonia's sharp peaks, steep valleys, and broad plains are continually changed by weathering, mass wasting, and erosion. These processes are related, but they describe different parts of landscape change.

Understanding the process

Weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces or changes its minerals. Physical weathering can happen when water enters cracks, freezes, and expands. Chemical weathering changes minerals through reactions with water, oxygen, or weak acids. Erosion is the movement of sediment by water, wind, ice, or gravity.

Why it matters in Patagonia

Mass wasting is the downslope movement of rock and soil under gravity. Landslides and rockfalls can occur on steep slopes, especially when material becomes unstable. In mountain environments, freeze-thaw cycles, heavy rain, and steep relief can all contribute to slope change.

Glaciers also erode bedrock as they move. They can scrape, pluck, and transport rock, leaving valleys and deposits behind. The landscape around Torres del Paine carries evidence of ancient glacial activity, including valleys and moraines.

Three processes to tell apart

Weathering = breakdown · Erosion = transport · Mass wasting = downslope movement

A useful way to remember the difference: weathering breaks material down, erosion transports it, and mass wasting moves it downslope mainly because of gravity.
