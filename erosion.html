03 / Weathering, Mass Wasting & Erosion

How water, wind, ice, and gravity shape Chile

Chile has very different landscapes from north to south. The Atacama is extremely dry, central Chile has a Mediterranean climate, and Patagonia has mountains, forests, rivers, and large ice fields. Because of these differences, erosion does not happen in the same way everywhere. In Patagonia, glaciers, rain, rivers, and steep slopes are especially important.

A quick way to remember the terms: Weathering breaks down rock in place. Erosion moves sediment. Mass wasting moves rock or soil downhill mainly because of gravity.

1. Weathering and mass wasting

Physical weathering is common in the Andes and Patagonia. Water can enter cracks in rock and freeze during cold conditions. When it freezes, it expands and can make the cracks wider. Repeated freezing and thawing slowly breaks rock into smaller pieces. Temperature changes and the growth of ice and salt crystals can also weaken exposed rock.

Chemical weathering also happens, although its speed depends on the rock and the amount of water. Hydrolysis occurs when water reacts with minerals such as feldspar and changes them into clay minerals. Carbonation happens when carbon dioxide mixes with water to form a weak acid that can dissolve carbonate minerals. These reactions are more effective where water is available, such as wetter parts of southern Chile. In the dry Atacama, chemical weathering is generally slower because liquid water is scarce.

Mass wasting is important in Chile because the Andes have steep slopes and the country experiences earthquakes, heavy rain, and periods of snow and ice melt. Landslides, rockfalls, debris flows, and mudflows can block roads, damage houses, affect farms, and interrupt access to remote communities. Rainfall can saturate soil, while earthquakes can shake loose rock and sediment.

A major example: the 1960 southern Chile earthquake

The magnitude 9.5 earthquake of May 22, 1960 triggered numerous major landslides and hundreds of smaller, shallow slope failures in southern Chile. The USGS documents these failures as part of the earthquake's effects. The landslides show how strong ground shaking can destabilize steep slopes and move large amounts of soil and rock, even without rainfall being the immediate trigger.

Lago Riñihue is associated with two major earthquake-related landslide events. In December 1575, a landslide blocked the lake outlet; after nearly four months, the natural dam failed and a catastrophic flood struck Valdivia. In May 1960, another earthquake-triggered group of landslides blocked the outlet. The lake rose about 26.5 m above its normal level before engineers cut a controlled channel and released the water in July 1960. The two events are distinct, and together they show the flood risk created when landslides temporarily dam a lake. Today, Chile also uses hazard mapping, monitoring, emergency planning, and public warnings. SERNAGEOMIN maintains a national inventory of mass-movement events and identifies triggers such as rain, earthquakes, thaw, and erosion at the base of slopes.

2. Water erosion and Chile's rivers

Water erosion occurs at different scales in Chile. Rain can cause splash, sheet, rill, and gully erosion on exposed soil, especially on slopes where vegetation has been removed. Rivers also erode their beds and banks. They pick up sediment, carry it downstream, and deposit it where the current slows. This process can change channels, create gravel bars and islands, and sometimes threaten roads, bridges, farmland, and buildings near the river.

Chile's rivers usually begin in the Andes. In the north, many are short and have low or highly variable flows. Farther south, rainfall and snowmelt feed larger rivers. The government’s annual environmental report lists the lengths and monitoring stations of major rivers. Discharge changes with season, rainfall, snowmelt, and the location of the measuring station, so the values are not directly comparable unless the station is the same.

How river erosion affects people

The Biobío is important for hydroelectric power, agriculture, industry, and communities in south-central Chile. Changes in flow and sediment can affect riverbanks, bridges, water infrastructure, and habitats. Along the Baker, the river and its surrounding wetlands are important for Patagonia's ecosystems, tourism, and local rural life. In the north, the Loa is especially valuable because it supplies water in a very dry region; erosion and sediment can affect water quality and irrigation systems.

Rivers in Chile do not all have one channel shape. Mountain sections are often narrow and confined. Some foothill and valley sections are braided, with several channels separated by gravel bars. Other stretches are sinuous or meandering. The Baker is a good example of this variety. A geomorphological study documented changes in channel pattern along the river, including confined, semi-confined, and braided reaches, as well as gravel bars and islands.

What is the government doing?

Chile's Ministry of Public Works, through the Dirección de Obras Hidráulicas (DOH), plans and builds river works to protect communities and infrastructure from floods and channel erosion. These can include bank protection, levees, channel improvements, and flood-control works. The DOH also prepares river-channel management plans that identify areas exposed to flooding or erosion. The Dirección General de Aguas (DGA) regulates work in natural channels and can require measures to protect people and property. These projects can reduce risk, but they also need to consider river habitats and natural channel movement.

Sources: DOH, River Works and Alluvial Control; DGA, authorization of river-channel works.

3. Wind erosion

Yes. Wind erosion is a concern in parts of Chile, especially in the dry north and in open areas of the Patagonian steppe. When soil is dry and has little plant cover, strong winds can lift fine particles and move sand. This removes the most fertile surface material, can damage young plants, and creates dust. In Patagonia, overgrazing and loss of vegetation can leave soil more exposed to the wind.

Wind erosion is not equally important across the country. In the Atacama, there is very little vegetation in many places, but some surfaces are protected by gravel, rock, or a hard desert crust. In other areas, dunes and loose sand are moved by wind. In the steppe, keeping grasses and shrubs healthy is important because their roots hold soil in place.

One specific government measure is the SIRSD-S soil recovery program, coordinated by Chile's Ministry of Agriculture and carried out through the Agricultural and Livestock Service (SAG) and INDAP. It provides financial support for practices such as restoring plant cover on bare or damaged soil and using soil-management methods that reduce soil loss and erosion. These actions can also help reduce wind erosion because vegetation protects the soil surface. The program is focused on agricultural soils, so it is not a solution for every desert or natural steppe area.

Program information: SAG, Soil Recovery Program; INDAP, SIRSD-S.

4. Deserts and desertification

The Atacama Desert dominates northern Chile. It is one of the driest places on Earth. The landscape includes rocky slopes, gravel plains, salt flats, dry valleys, and sand dunes. The Andes and coastal mountain ranges help create the region's extreme dryness. Even here, occasional rainfall and rare storms can cause flash floods and move large amounts of sediment.

Other dry landscapes include the coastal desert around the Norte Chico and the semi-arid transition zone toward central Chile. Farther south, the Patagonian steppe is not usually classified as a true hot desert, but it is dry and windy, with grasslands and shrublands that can be vulnerable to land degradation.

Desert landscapes: erg, reg, and hamada

Erg — sand

Areas of dunes and loose sand shaped by wind. Chile has dune fields in parts of the Atacama and along sections of the northern coast.

Reg — gravel

Stony or gravel-covered desert surfaces. These are common across broad parts of the Atacama, where wind removes finer particles and leaves coarser material behind.

Hamada — rock

Exposed rocky plateaus and bedrock surfaces. Rocky hills and bare outcrops are widespread in the Atacama landscape.

Salt flats and basins

Salars, such as the Salar de Atacama, are closed basins where water evaporates and leaves salts and fine sediments. They are not an erg, reg, or hamada, but are a major desert landform.

Desertification is a concern in Chile, particularly in dry and semi-arid regions where drought, soil erosion, vegetation loss, and pressure from land use can reduce the quality of the land. It does not mean that every desert is expanding in the same way. It means that land in dry areas can lose its ability to support vegetation and productive uses. In the north and central parts of Chile, water scarcity makes recovery difficult. In parts of Patagonia, heavy grazing and vegetation loss can make soils more vulnerable to wind erosion.

For an overview of the Atacama's desert and high-altitude landscapes, see Chile Travel. NASA's satellite explanation of the Salar de Atacama shows how water still shapes this very dry environment.

5. Glaciers: present and ancient

Glaciers have had a major influence on Chile, especially in Patagonia. During past ice ages, large ice fields covered much of the southern Andes. Moving ice scraped and plucked bedrock, widened valleys, and transported huge amounts of sediment. When the ice retreated, it left U-shaped valleys, fjords, deep lakes, moraines, and other deposits. The dramatic mountain and lake scenery around Torres del Paine is part of this glacial landscape.

Chile still has many glaciers today. The DGA's 2022 Public Glacier Inventory recorded 26,169 glaciers in continental Chile, covering 21,009.8 km² (about 2.8% of the national territory when Chilean Antarctica is excluded). The inventory estimates 2,710.7 km³ of ice. The austral macrozone, Aysén and Magallanes, contains most of the mapped glacier area. Examples include Grey Glacier in Torres del Paine National Park and the glaciers of the Northern and Southern Patagonian Ice Fields.

Glaciers are important because they store freshwater and release meltwater into rivers, especially during warmer months. This water supports ecosystems, communities, agriculture, and some energy production. Glacial lakes and steep valleys can also create hazards when water is suddenly released or when unstable slopes collapse near the ice. At the same time, glacier retreat changes landscapes and can affect water availability over time.

The DGA reported that the 2022 inventory showed an approximately 8% reduction in mapped glacier area compared with the 2014 inventory. The agency also explains that the two inventories used improved methods and newer, higher-resolution satellite images, so the comparison should be read with that methodological change in mind. The DGA maintains the national inventory and monitoring information used to track glacier conditions.

Sources: DGA Public Glacier Inventory; Fundación Glaciares Chilenos, current glacier conditions.
