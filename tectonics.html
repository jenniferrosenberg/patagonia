02 / Plate Tectonics, Earthquakes & Volcanoes

Chile: a landscape shaped by moving plates

Chile lies along one of the most active tectonic margins on Earth. The movement of oceanic and continental plates has helped build the Andes, created a chain of volcanoes, and produced some of the strongest earthquakes ever recorded. These same geological processes also helped form valuable mineral deposits that are important to Chile's economy.

1. Tectonic plates and natural disasters

Most of Chile's mainland is next to a convergent plate boundary. Offshore, the oceanic Nazca Plate moves eastward and sinks beneath the continental South American Plate along the Peru–Chile Trench. This process is called subduction. The plates converge at roughly 65–80 millimeters per year along different parts of the margin. Friction can lock sections of the boundary, allowing stress to build until it is released as an earthquake (Hayes et al., 2015).

Subduction also helps explain the Andes and the volcanic chain that runs through parts of Chile. Water released from the descending plate contributes to melting in the mantle above it; magma can then rise and feed volcanoes. Not every part of the boundary has the same volcanic activity: where the Nazca Plate descends at a very shallow angle beneath central Chile, active volcanoes are less common (Hayes et al., 2015).

Chile also sits near a complex plate junction in the far south. Around the Chile Triple Junction, the Antarctic, South American, and Nazca plates meet; farther south and east, the Scotia Plate interacts with the South American Plate. These boundaries help explain why southern Patagonia and Tierra del Fuego have a different tectonic setting from central and northern Chile (Hayes et al., 2015).

2. Rocks, minerals, and Chile's economy

Chile has important igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks, but its best-known mineral wealth is connected to the Andes and the long history of magmatism and hydrothermal activity. Copper deposits, including large porphyry copper systems, are especially important. Chile also produces lithium from brines in salt flats, as well as molybdenum, iodine, gold, silver, and iron ore.

Mining is a major part of the national economy. In 2024, the mineral sector represented about 7.2% of Chile's gross domestic product and approximately 61% of the value of the country's exports. Chile was the world's leading producer of mined copper, accounting for 24% of global production, and the second-largest lithium producer in the USGS estimate (U.S. Geological Survey [USGS], 2025).

Mining creates direct jobs in exploration, engineering, geology, mine operations, transportation, maintenance, and processing. It also supports indirect employment in construction, equipment, ports, energy, and other services. These jobs are important in northern mining regions, although the industry is capital-intensive and employment can change with commodity prices and investment cycles.

Chile's leading copper export destinations in 2024 were China (about 52% of copper exports), the United States (11%), Japan (10%), and South Korea (6%) (USGS, 2025). Copper is used in electrical wiring, power grids, construction, and electronics. Lithium is used mainly in rechargeable batteries, including those in electric vehicles and energy-storage systems.

Environmental and social impacts

Mining also creates challenges. Copper mining can produce large volumes of waste rock and tailings, use substantial energy, and place pressure on water resources in dry northern regions. Lithium is extracted from brines in salt flats such as the Salar de Atacama. The OECD (2024) explains that pumping and evaporating brines can affect groundwater connections and may place pressure on freshwater availability, wetlands, wildlife, and local communities. Mining therefore brings export income and employment, but it also requires careful water management, environmental monitoring, consultation, and rehabilitation.

3. Earthquakes and preparedness

Chile experiences frequent earthquakes because the Nazca and South American plates are continuously converging. Large earthquakes can occur when a locked section of the plate boundary suddenly slips. If the seafloor moves vertically, the earthquake can also generate a tsunami.

May 22, 1960 — Valdivia, magnitude 9.5The largest earthquake recorded instrumentally. It caused destructive shaking and a Pacific-wide tsunami that reached places including Hawaii and Japan (Biblioteca Nacional de Chile, n.d.).
February 27, 2010 — Maule, magnitude 8.8The offshore megathrust earthquake ruptured a long section of the plate boundary and generated a tsunami that affected coastal communities (USGS, 2010).
September 16, 2015 — Illapel, magnitude 8.3A major offshore earthquake in northern-central Chile generated a tsunami and prompted coastal evacuations (USGS, 2015).

What does the government do to prepare people?

Chile's National Service for Disaster Prevention and Response (SENAPRED) provides public guidance, preparedness education, and emergency information. Its recommendations include identifying safe places in homes, schools, and workplaces; securing heavy furniture; knowing evacuation routes; and preparing an emergency kit. For coastal communities, tsunami evacuation routes and designated safe areas are important. The Hydrographic and Oceanographic Service of the Chilean Navy (SHOA) monitors tsunami threats and issues technical tsunami information for authorities.

Chile also uses seismic design standards for buildings. These standards do not prevent earthquakes, but they are intended to reduce structural damage and protect lives. Preparation still matters: people should follow official alerts and evacuate when authorities instruct them to do so (SENAPRED, n.d.).

Earthquake safety

Know your safe place and your evacuation route

During strong shaking, protect yourself from falling objects. If you are on the coast and the earthquake is so strong or long that it is difficult to stand, move to higher ground immediately rather than waiting for a tsunami message. Always follow official local instructions.

4. Mountain ranges and their influence

Chile's mountains are not just scenery. They influence climate, water, transportation, settlement, and culture. The country's main north–south landforms include the Andes in the east and the Cordillera de la Costa near the Pacific, with the Central Valley or Intermediate Depression between them. In northern Chile, the Domeyko Range is an important mountain system west of the high Andes and is closely associated with the region's mineral landscapes (Ministerio del Medio Ambiente, 2016).

The Andes Mountains

The Andes are a long, high mountain chain formed largely through the convergence and subduction of oceanic plates beneath South America. In northern Chile, many peaks rise above 6,000 meters. The range includes volcanic peaks, high plateaus, deep valleys, and glaciated areas farther south. It forms a major international boundary with Argentina and Bolivia and contains important headwaters for rivers and streams.

The Cordillera de la Costa

This mountain system runs roughly parallel to the Pacific coast. Its height and shape vary along the country: in northern Chile, sections rise steeply above the coastal plain, while in central and southern Chile the range changes in elevation and continuity. It influences local coastal climates and separates some coastal areas from the Central Valley.

The Domeyko Range and the Patagonian Andes

The Domeyko Range is an important north–south mountain system in the Atacama region, west of the high Andes. It is part of the geological setting of northern Chile's mineral districts. Farther south, the Andes become more deeply dissected by glaciers and fjords. In Patagonia, the Southern Andes include ice fields, sharp peaks, and mountain valleys that strongly shape the landscape around Torres del Paine.

Climate, water, culture, and trade

The Andes create a rain-shadow effect that contributes to the extreme dryness of the Atacama region. In central Chile, snow stored in the Andes melts seasonally and helps supply rivers used for drinking water, irrigation, and hydropower. Farther south, abundant precipitation, glaciers, lakes, and rivers shape forests and communities. Mountains also influence local livelihoods and culture, including pastoralism, tourism, mountaineering, and Indigenous relationships with highland landscapes (NASA, 2023).

Historically, steep mountains could make travel and trade difficult, but they were not an absolute barrier. Passes through the Andes connected communities and supported exchange between the Pacific and the interior of South America. Today, mountain passes, highways, tunnels, and border crossings connect Chile with Argentina and Bolivia. The Andes therefore function both as a natural barrier and as a corridor where routes are possible.

5. Volcanoes in Chile

Chile has many volcanoes because subduction generates magma beneath the Andes. The Smithsonian Institution's Global Volcanism Program lists 90 Holocene volcanoes associated with Chile. Many are composite volcanoes (stratovolcanoes), built from alternating layers of lava and ash. Examples include Villarrica, Llaima, and Calbuco. Other volcanic systems have different forms, including volcanic complexes and caldera systems (Global Volcanism Program, n.d.).

VillarricaA prominent composite volcano in southern Chile. Its activity can produce ash, lava, and lahars from melting snow and ice.
LlaimaA large composite volcano in the Southern Andes. Its eruptions have produced lava and ash and can affect nearby communities and transport.
CalbucoA composite volcano in the Los Lagos region. Its explosive 2015 eruption sent ash high into the atmosphere and disrupted nearby communities and air travel.
ChaiténA volcano that produced a major explosive eruption in 2008, followed by dome growth. Ashfall and lahars affected the town and led to a large evacuation.
LáscarAn active composite volcano in northern Chile, known for explosive eruptions and ash emissions.
Nevados de ChillánA volcanic complex with a prolonged period of activity beginning in 2016, including ash and lava-dome activity.

How do volcanoes affect people and the environment?

Volcanic activity creates hazards, including ashfall, lava flows, pyroclastic density currents, lahars, and gases. Ash can affect breathing, water supplies, crops, roads, and aircraft. Lahars are especially dangerous when volcanic material mixes with rainwater or melted snow and ice and flows down valleys.

Volcanoes can also bring benefits. Volcanic materials weather into mineral-rich soils that can support agriculture, and volcanic landscapes attract visitors for hiking, hot springs, skiing, and scenic tourism. Geothermal energy is another possible resource. The challenge is to enjoy and use these landscapes while respecting hazard zones and evacuation guidance.

Examples of historical eruptions

  • Chaitén, 2008: A major explosive eruption began on May 2, 2008. Ashfall affected southern Chile and neighboring Argentina, and thousands of residents were evacuated.
  • Puyehue–Cordón Caulle, 2011: The eruption began in June 2011 and continued into 2012. Ash spread over a wide area, disrupted air travel, and affected communities and livestock.
  • Calbuco, 2015: Explosive activity began on April 22, 2015. Ashfall affected towns in the Los Lagos region and led to evacuations and travel disruption.
  • Villarrica, 2015: An explosive eruption in March 2015 prompted evacuations near the volcano and highlighted the risk to nearby communities and tourism areas.

SERNAGEOMIN's National Volcanic Surveillance Network monitors volcanoes and communicates activity information. The Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program provides a scientific record of volcanoes and their eruptions. These sources help authorities and communities understand changing volcanic conditions.
